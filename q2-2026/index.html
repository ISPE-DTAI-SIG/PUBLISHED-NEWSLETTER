Welcome

Welcome to the Q2 2026 edition of the DTAI SIG newsletter. The second quarter was one of the most exciting stretches for frontier AI this year. Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google all shipped major model releases, including the first publicly available Mythos-class model from Anthropic and a new default model for ChatGPT. We cover the releases that matter most for our community in a new Frontier Model Watch section this issue.

The quarter also produced a study that has many questioning the reliability of domain tailored models. A Nature Medicine evaluation found that general-purpose frontier models outperformed two widely marketed clinical AI tools across medical knowledge, clinician alignment, and real clinical queries. The finding speaks directly to the need for benchmarking and validating models especially if they are used to benefit patients in any way.

Closer to home, we held our inaugural Research Day on April 23, convened our all-SIG meeting on May 7 hosted by the GenAI Working Group, and kicked off our new Agentic AI working group being led by Emaan Rashidi (JHU).

Looking ahead, the 42nd ISPE Annual Meeting runs August 29 to September 2 in Milan, and the DTAI SIG will have a strong presence. I’m extremely proud of all the work we’ve been doing and looking forward to a great meeting!

Frontier Model Watch: Q2 2026

The second quarter delivered an unusually dense run of frontier model releases. The summary below is oriented to what matters for pharmacoepidemiology, pharmacovigilance, and RWE.

Anthropic

Anthropic shipped four models in the quarter. Claude Opus 4.7 (April 16) and Claude Opus 4.8 (May 28) improved coding, agentic reliability, and vision, with Opus 4.8 emphasizing honesty and self-verification. On June 9, Anthropic released Claude Fable 5, the first publicly available Mythos-class model, alongside the restricted Claude Mythos 5. Anthropic describes the Mythos class as sitting above its Opus class in capability. Fable 5 ships with safety classifiers that route flagged requests in areas such as cybersecurity and biology to Claude Opus 4.8. Mythos 5 is the same underlying model without those classifiers and is limited to vetted cyberdefenders through Project Glasswing.

On June 12, Anthropic suspended access to both Mythos-class models to comply with a U.S. Department of Commerce export-control directive. The Department lifted the controls on June 30, and Anthropic restored access on July 1. Access to the Opus, Sonnet, and Haiku models was unaffected throughout. Claude Sonnet 5, a lower-cost agentic model, followed on June 30.

OpenAI

OpenAI released GPT-5.5 on April 23, emphasizing agentic coding, computer use, and knowledge work. GPT-5.5 Instant became the default ChatGPT model for all users on May 5, with OpenAI reporting roughly 52 percent fewer hallucinated claims than the prior default on high-stakes prompts in medicine, law, and finance. OpenAI previewed the GPT-5.6 family (Sol, Terra, and Luna) to a limited set of partners on June 26 under a U.S. government safety review, with general availability following in early July.

Google

Google continued to iterate the Gemini 3 family. Gemini 3.1 Pro, released in February, remained its flagship reasoning model through much of the quarter and features in the Nature Medicine study covered below. Google also moved additional Gemini 3.5 Flash and managed-agent platform capabilities to general availability.

Why it matters

Two themes stand out for our field. First, cybersecurity and biology capability now drives release strategy as well as safety concerns. Both Anthropic and OpenAI gated their most capable models behind classifiers, trusted-access programs, or government review. Second, the models are increasingly built for long-horizon, multi-step agentic work, which makes our agentic AI workstream critically important.

Featured Study

Frontier models outperform specialized clinical AI tools

Vishwanath K, Alyakin A, Ghosh M, et al. General-purpose large language models outperform specialized clinical AI tools on medical benchmarks. Nat Med. 2026. doi:10.1038/s41591-026-04431-5

A team led by NYU Langone Health published an independent evaluation comparing two specialized clinical AI tools, OpenEvidence and UpToDate Expert AI, against three general-purpose frontier models: GPT-5.2, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and Claude Opus 4.6. The evaluation ran in three stages: 500 MedQA questions for medical knowledge, 500 HealthBench items for clinician alignment, and a real clinical queries benchmark built from 100 de-identified physician queries drawn from a live, HIPAA-compliant deployment. Twelve U.S. clinicians reviewed model outputs under randomized, blinded conditions, producing 1,800 model-question annotations.

Findings

The frontier models outperformed the specialized clinical tools on all three evaluations. On the real clinical queries benchmark, a clear two-tier structure emerged, with the three frontier models above the clinical tools. Notably, the specialized tools performed comparably to Google Search AI Overview, an auto-enabled consumer search feature. Safety outcomes did not differ across models. No system produced significantly more harmful content or hallucinations than any other.

Caveats the authors raise

The team is careful about scope. Clinical tools were queried through browser interfaces because they lack public APIs, which limited sample size. Standard benchmarks carry contamination risk, and HealthBench is an OpenAI-developed instrument, so the authors treat the blinded clinician evaluation as their primary evidence. They frame the results as a snapshot of a fast-moving landscape rather than a permanent ordering, and note that deeply subspecialized tasks and hospital-grounded, institution-specific models may still favor domain-specific approaches.

Why it matters for our community

This is a great example of a head to head comparison that is needed for LLMs and agentic systems. Today, there are hundreds of industry and vendor claims that their AI tools are superior to others but what’s the gold standard? For pharmacoepidemiology and pharmacovigilance, where tools are marketed for writing protocols and other documents, signal detection, literature surveillance, and evidence synthesis, without a targeted validation procedure, these claims are arguably weak. In this manuscript, the authors conclude that the results “highlight the need for independent, real-world evaluation” before tools enter clinical settings. That is exactly the focus of our agentic AI workstream.
